To write a video script, decide the one thing the viewer should take away, write a hook that names their problem, give two or three points with an example for each, and end with one next step. Then read it aloud, cut every line you would not say in conversation, and check the length: at about 150 words per minute, a 60-second video needs roughly 150 words.

This guide is for videos where you talk to the camera: Reels, Shorts, TikTok, YouTube explainers, course lessons and product walkthroughs. If you are writing a film or a scene with several characters, you need screenplay format instead, which this guide does not cover.

## The 5 steps at a glance

1. **Write one sentence about the viewer and the takeaway.** "A new creator whose phone videos sound echoey will know three fixes they can try tonight."
2. **Choose a full script or talking points.** Use the table below.
3. **Draft in four beats:** hook, promise, points, close.
4. **Format it for reading on camera:** one idea per line, short sentences, pauses marked.
5. **Read it aloud and cut to length.** Time yourself and trim until it fits.

## Step 1: Write the brief in one sentence

Every other decision depends on this sentence, so write it before the script:

> [Specific viewer] will know/be able to [one change] after watching.

If you need two sentences, you probably have two videos. Keep the second as a separate idea. A narrow brief also makes the hook easier, because the hook only has to promise that one change.

## Step 2: Decide between a full script and talking points

A word-for-word script and a list of talking points are both valid. They suit different videos.

| Write a full script when                                   | Use talking points when                           |
| ---------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| Exact wording matters: prices, legal terms, product claims | You know the subject well and explain it often    |
| The video is short and every second counts                 | The video is long and conversational              |
| You tend to ramble or lose the thread                      | Reading makes you sound flat, even after practice |
| You will read from a teleprompter                          | You will glance at a note between sections        |
| Several people need to approve the wording first           | You can fix structure in the edit                 |

You can also mix the two: a word-for-word hook and close, with talking points in the middle. The opening and ending are where wording matters most, and the middle is where reading tends to sound stiff.

## Step 3: Draft the four beats

Short talking-to-camera videos work well with four parts.

**Hook.** Name the viewer's problem or the result in the first sentence. Skip greetings and channel introductions. YouTube's own guidance says a strong opening should deliver on what the title and thumbnail promised and keep the intro concise ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/16559650)). In YouTube Studio, the "Intro" key moment shows what percentage of viewers are still watching 30 seconds in, so you can check whether your hook works on videos of at least 60 seconds ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314415)).

**Promise.** One sentence that tells the viewer what they are about to get: "Here are three fixes you can make tonight."

**Points.** Two or three, each with a concrete example, number or demonstration. If a point has no example, add one or cut the point.

**Close.** One next step. Ask for one thing: try the fix, watch the next video, or comment with a specific answer.

## Step 4: Format the script for reading on camera

A script that reads well on paper can still trip you up on a teleprompter. Format it for your mouth:

- **One idea per line.** Break lines where you would breathe.
- **Short sentences.** If a sentence has more than one comma, split it.
- **Mark pauses.** Leave a blank line or write `[pause]` where you want a beat.
- **Spell out what you will say.** Write "about a hand's width" instead of a measurement you would have to convert in your head, and write numbers the way you say them.
- **Add stage notes in brackets.** `[hold up the mic]` or `[cut to screen]` reminds you what to show without being read aloud.

Here is the same opening written two ways:

```text
Paragraph version (hard to read on camera):
If your videos sound like you recorded them in a bathroom, the problem is usually the room and not your phone, so in this video I'm going to go through three fixes that you can make tonight without having to buy a new camera.

Teleprompter version:
If your videos sound like you recorded them in a bathroom,
the room is the problem.

[pause]

Here are three fixes you can make tonight.
No new camera.
```

The second version is shorter, has a natural pause, and each line is one breath.

## Step 5: Read it aloud and cut to length

Read the script aloud once at normal speed and time it. Mark any line where you stumble or that sounds like writing. Rewrite those lines the way you would say them to a friend, then read again.

To estimate length before you record, divide the word count by your speaking rate. The National Center for Voice and Speech puts average conversational English at about 150 words per minute ([NCVS](https://ncvs.org/tutorials/voice-qualities/)). Many people read a script more slowly than that, especially with pauses, so time yourself once and use your own number.

| Video length | Words at 120 wpm | Words at 150 wpm |
| ------------ | ---------------- | ---------------- |
| 30 seconds   | 60               | 75               |
| 60 seconds   | 120              | 150              |
| 90 seconds   | 180              | 225              |
| 3 minutes    | 360              | 450              |
| 8 minutes    | 960              | 1,200            |

Three minutes is a useful upper limit for short vertical video. YouTube Shorts can be up to three minutes long for uploads since October 15, 2024 ([YouTube Blog](https://blog.youtube/news-and-events/tall-updates-coming-to-shorts/)), and Instagram raised the reel limit to three minutes in January 2025 ([Instagram for Creators](https://creators.instagram.com/blog/the-latest-with-instagram)). Both are maximums. A script that only needs 45 seconds should stay at 45 seconds.

## A filled-in example: a script under one minute

This is an illustrative script, written for this guide. It shows the structure and word counts. It is not a transcript of a real video.

**Brief:** A new creator whose phone videos sound echoey will know three fixes they can try tonight.

| Beat    | Script                                                                                                                                                    |   Words |
| ------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------: |
| Hook    | If your videos sound like you recorded them in a bathroom, the room is the problem.                                                                       |      16 |
| Promise | Here are three fixes you can make tonight. No new camera.                                                                                                 |      11 |
| Point 1 | First, move the mic closer. If you are using your phone, it is probably an arm's length away. Bring it in, or clip a cheap lapel mic to your shirt.       |      30 |
| Point 2 | Second, add soft things. Bare walls bounce your voice back. Curtains, a rug, a sofa or a blanket over a chair behind the camera all soak some of that up. |      30 |
| Point 3 | Third, record a ten-second test and listen on headphones before you film the whole thing. Your laptop speakers will hide the echo.                        |      22 |
| Close   | Try the first fix today. If it still sounds hollow, comment with what your room looks like and I will suggest the next change.                            |      24 |
|         | **Total**                                                                                                                                                 | **133** |

At 150 words per minute, 133 words runs about 53 seconds. Each point has something the viewer can do, and the close asks for one specific action.

## Copy this video script template

```text
BRIEF
[Viewer in a specific situation] will know / be able to [one change].

HOOK (1–2 sentences, word for word)
[Name the problem or the result. No greeting.]

PROMISE (1 sentence)
[What they will get from this video.]

POINT 1
[Point] + [example, number or demonstration]
[stage note: what to show]

POINT 2
[Point] + [example]

POINT 3 (optional)
[Point] + [example]

CLOSE (1–2 sentences, word for word)
[One next step.]

LENGTH CHECK
Words: ___  ÷ my words per minute: ___  = ___ minutes
```

For a longer YouTube video, repeat the point block and add a one-line recap before the close. For a product walkthrough, make each point one step the viewer can follow on screen.

## Write it so recording and editing are easier

A few habits in the script save time later:

- **Leave a pause between sections.** A clean gap gives you a natural cut point if you redo a section.
- **Restart a line instead of the whole take.** When you stumble, pause and say the full line again. In a transcript-based editor you can then delete the failed attempt as text.
- **Keep names and terms consistent.** If you write a product or person's name the same way every time, it is easier to check the captions for spelling.
- **Write the on-screen text separately.** Captions show what you said. Titles and labels you add in the edit can be shorter.

## Common questions

### How long should a video script be?

Count words, not pages. At around 150 words per minute, plan about 75 words for 30 seconds, 150 for a minute and 450 for three minutes. Time yourself reading once, because your pace with pauses may be closer to 120 words per minute.

### How do I write a video script as a beginner?

Start with the template above and a 30- to 60-second video. Write the hook and close word for word and use talking points for the middle. Read it aloud before recording. A short script is easier to fix than a long one.

### Can ChatGPT or another AI write my video script?

It can write a first draft. Give it your brief, the viewer, the points you want to make and any facts or examples it should use, and tell it not to invent results or stories. Then check every claim and rewrite the lines in your own words. Read the draft aloud; AI drafts often have long sentences that are hard to say.

### What are the three C's of writing a good script?

There is no single official list. Versions you will see include clear, concise and compelling, or clear, concise and conversational. Used as a final check, they map to the steps here: one takeaway (clear), a word count that fits the length (concise), and lines you would say out loud (conversational).

### Should I memorize my script or use a teleprompter?

For a short video you know well, talking points or memorizing the hook and close is often enough. For longer or precise scripts, a teleprompter helps you stay accurate. Set the scroll speed slower than you think, keep the text near the lens, and do a short test take first. [What is a teleprompter and how does it work?](/blog/what-is-a-teleprompter) covers setups, scroll speed and eye-line in more detail.

### What is the difference between a video script and a screenplay?

A screenplay uses a fixed format for film and TV: scene headings, action lines and character names. A script for a talking-head or explainer video only needs what you will say, in order, with notes for what to show. Two-column scripts (words on one side, visuals on the other) sit between the two and are useful when you plan B-roll or screen recordings.

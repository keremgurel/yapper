A YouTube script has six parts: a hook, a promise, a short setup, the body split into chapters, a payoff and a closing line that leaves room for the end screen. Write the first 30 seconds and the last 20 seconds word for word, and use talking points for the middle unless you are giving steps or numbers. At a conversational 150 words per minute, a 10-minute video is about 1,500 spoken words, and 1,300 if you pause for demos.

This guide is for long-form YouTube videos where you talk to camera or over a screen recording. For the general method that works at any length, start with [how to write a video script](/blog/how-to-write-a-video-script). For under a minute, see [how to write a TikTok script](/blog/tiktok-script), which also works for Shorts. The example outline below is written for this guide and is illustrative, so replace its claims with your own.

## The six parts of a YouTube script

| Part     | Where it sits     | What it does                                                    | Write it…                |
| -------- | ----------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------ |
| Hook     | 0:00 to ~0:15     | Names the problem, the result or the question the video answers | Word for word            |
| Promise  | by ~0:30          | Says what the viewer will have by the end, matching the title   | Word for word            |
| Setup    | 30 to 60 seconds  | The context the viewer needs, and nothing more                  | Bullets                  |
| Chapters | Most of the video | Three to five sections, each opening with its own mini-hook     | Bullets or full, by type |
| Payoff   | Last 1–2 minutes  | Delivers what the hook promised and sums up in one or two lines | Bullets                  |
| Close    | Last 5–20 seconds | One next step, timed to sit under the end screen                | Word for word            |

**Hook.** Start with the reason someone clicked. Skip "Hey guys, welcome back to the channel". If the title is "How to light a room with one lamp", the first sentence should be about lighting with one lamp.

**Promise.** One sentence that lists what is coming. This is where the title and thumbnail get confirmed or broken. YouTube's own audience retention report has an "Intro" figure, the percentage of viewers still watching after the first 30 seconds, and YouTube's advice for a low figure is to make the first 30 seconds match what the title and thumbnail promised ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314415)).

**Setup.** Only what the viewer needs to follow the first chapter. History, your backstory and the sponsor read do not belong here.

**Chapters.** Each chapter makes one point and starts with a line that gives the viewer a reason to keep watching: a question, the next problem or a hint of the result. This line is the bridge from the last section into the next.

**Payoff.** Show or say the result the hook promised. If you promised three fixes, the viewer should be able to count three.

**Close.** One action. End screens can sit on the last 5 to 20 seconds of a video ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/6388789)), so script a closing line that gives you that time without dead air, such as pointing to the next video you will put on the end screen.

## How many words is a YouTube script?

Multiply the minutes by your speaking rate. The National Center for Voice and Speech puts average conversational English at about 150 words per minute ([NCVS](https://ncvs.org/tutorials/voice-qualities/)). Screen recordings and demos have gaps where you are clicking or showing, so plan at 130 for those.

| Video length | Words at 130 wpm (demos, pauses) | Words at 150 wpm (talking to camera) |
| ------------ | -------------------------------: | -----------------------------------: |
| 5 minutes    |                              650 |                                  750 |
| 8 minutes    |                            1,040 |                                1,200 |
| 10 minutes   |                            1,300 |                                1,500 |
| 12 minutes   |                            1,560 |                                1,800 |
| 15 minutes   |                            1,950 |                                2,250 |
| 20 minutes   |                            2,600 |                                3,000 |

Measure your own rate once: read 150 words of your script aloud with a timer and divide. Then budget the script by part. For a 10-minute talking-head video at 150 words per minute, a workable split is about 75 words for hook and promise, 100 for setup, 1,150 across four chapters (roughly 290 each), 125 for the payoff and 50 for the close.

Let the content set the length. If the point is made in seven minutes, a seven-minute video is better than a ten-minute one padded to fit.

## Full script or bullet points?

Creators on r/NewTubers ask this constantly, and the answers in threads like ["Is it better to write out a script word for word? Or make bullet points"](https://www.reddit.com/r/NewTubers/comments/18o6hlk/is_it_better_to_write_out_a_script_word_for_word/) and ["Write out videos or go with the flow"](https://www.reddit.com/r/NewTubers/comments/1whcg34/write_out_videos_or_go_with_the_flow/) mostly land on the same compromise: script the opening (and often the ending) word for word, then use ordered talking points for the body. People who script everything say it stops them rambling; people who use bullets say they sound more natural. Both are right for different videos.

| Video type                   | Hook and close  | Body                                                  |
| ---------------------------- | --------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| Tutorial or screen recording | Word for word   | Full script for each step, so nothing is skipped      |
| Video essay or explainer     | Word for word   | Full script; precise wording and sources matter       |
| Review or comparison         | Word for word   | Bullets with the exact claims and numbers written out |
| Opinion, commentary or story | Word for word   | Three to five keywords per chapter                    |
| Vlog or behind the scenes    | First line only | A shot list instead of a script                       |

If you use a full script, write the way you talk and read each line out loud before recording. If you would need a breath in the middle of a sentence, split it.

## Copy this YouTube script template

Two columns help on long videos: what you say, and what the viewer sees. The chapter titles double as the timestamps you will paste into the description.

```text
TITLE (working):
THUMBNAIL TEXT:
ONE-LINE BRIEF: [Viewer in a specific situation] will be able to [one result] by the end.

──────── HOOK  (0:00, word for word, 1–2 sentences) ────────
SAY:  [The problem, result or question. No greeting.]
SHOW: [What is on screen in the first 5 seconds]

──────── PROMISE  (by 0:30, word for word, 1 sentence) ────────
SAY:  [What they will have by the end. Match the title.]

──────── SETUP  (bullets, under 60 seconds) ────────
- [Only the context needed for chapter 1]

──────── CHAPTER 1: [title for the description timestamp] ────────
MINI-HOOK: [Question or next problem that opens the chapter]
- [Point]
- [Example, number or demo]
SHOW: [B-roll, screen recording, graphic]

──────── CHAPTER 2: [title] ────────
MINI-HOOK:
- [Point]
- [Example]
SHOW:

──────── CHAPTER 3: [title] ────────
(repeat; most 8–15 minute videos need 3 to 5 chapters)

──────── PAYOFF  (bullets) ────────
- [The result the hook promised, shown or stated]
- [One- or two-line recap]

──────── CLOSE  (word for word, leave 5–20 s for the end screen) ────────
SAY:  [One next step, e.g. the video you will put on the end screen]

LENGTH CHECK
Spoken words: ____ ÷ my words per minute: ____ = ____ minutes
```

## A worked example: outline for a 10-minute video

**Brief:** New creators who record at home will be able to set up a phone as a teleprompter and read without looking like they are reading.

**Working title:** "How to use a teleprompter without looking like you're reading"

**Hook and promise (word for word, 61 words):**

> You can tell when someone is reading off a screen. Their eyes slide left to right, their voice goes flat and they stop blinking. You don't need to memorize your script to fix that. In the next ten minutes I'll show you where to put the text, how fast to scroll it and how to write lines you can actually say.

**Setup (bullets, ~100 words):** who this is for (phone or laptop, no rig), what you need (the script, a stand, a notes or teleprompter app), what we won't cover (hardware beam-splitter prompters).

**Chapter 1: Put the text next to the lens (~290 words).** Mini-hook: "The first fix costs nothing." Points: keep the text within an inch or two of the lens; narrow the column so your eyes move less; test by recording 20 seconds and watching your eyes. Show: side-by-side frames of text far from and near the lens.

**Chapter 2: Set the size and scroll speed (~290 words).** Mini-hook: "Most people scroll too fast and then rush to keep up." Points: three to five words per line; start slower than you think; pause the scroll at section breaks. Show: screen recording of the prompter settings.

**Chapter 3: Rewrite lines you trip on (~290 words).** Mini-hook: "If a line trips you up on the prompter, the problem is usually the line." Points: split long sentences; replace written words with spoken ones; mark where you will pause. Show: before-and-after version of one paragraph.

**Chapter 4: Record in sections (~290 words).** Mini-hook: "You don't have to get ten minutes right in one take." Points: one chapter per take; pause before and after each; restart the sentence, not the video. Show: the edit timeline with clean cut points.

**Payoff (~125 words):** play a clip recorded with all four fixes; one-line recap of the four.

**Close (word for word, 30 words):**

> If your script is the part that still sounds stiff, watch this next video on writing scripts that sound like you talking. It's on screen now. I'll see you there.

That outline totals about 1,480 words at the budgets above, just under ten minutes at 150 words per minute. The chapter titles become the description timestamps, which YouTube requires to start at 00:00, number at least three and run at least 10 seconds each ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9884579)).

For the deeper version of chapter 1, see [what is a teleprompter and how does it work](/blog/what-is-a-teleprompter). For the alternative to reading at all, see [how to memorize a script](/blog/how-to-memorize-a-script).

## Write for YouTube's features while you script

A few YouTube features are easier to plan in the script than to bolt on in the edit:

- **Chapters.** Write a short title for each chapter in the script. After the edit, copy the start times and titles into the description. The first timestamp must be 00:00, you need at least three and each chapter must be at least 10 seconds long.
- **End screens.** They can appear on the last 5 to 20 seconds, need a video of at least 25 seconds and can show up to four elements on a 16:9 video ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/6388789)). Your closing line should point at whatever you will put there.
- **Retention after you publish.** The audience retention report highlights dips (where viewers skipped or left) and spikes (where they rewatched or shared) once a video is at least 60 seconds long with at least 100 views ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314415)). Note the timestamps of the biggest dip and find that line in your script. That is the section to restructure in the next video.

## Using a YouTube script writer or AI generator

An AI script writer is useful for a first draft when the page is blank and for trying several hooks against the same title. It is weak at facts, at your own experience and at sentences that sound spoken. Treat its output as a draft:

- **Check every claim.** AI drafts invent statistics, studies and quotes. Delete anything you cannot source.
- **Cut the throat-clearing.** "In today's video we're going to dive into" is a common AI opener. Start with the hook.
- **Add one thing only you know.** A real example from your own work or a mistake you made is what separates your video from the ten others with the same title.
- **Read it aloud and count.** Compare the spoken word count with the table above, and split any line you need a breath for.

## Common mistakes in YouTube scripts

- **A long intro before the point.** The first 30 seconds should confirm the title.
- **Chapters that start cold.** Open each one with a question or the next problem.
- **The best part saved for the end.** YouTube's retention guidance suggests moving compelling material earlier, because audience size drops over a video.
- **Writing for the eye.** Long sentences and words you would never say out loud.
- **Padding to hit a length.** A tighter video beats a longer one with a slow middle.
- **Three calls to action.** Pick one, and make it the thing on your end screen.

## Common questions

### How do you write a script for a YouTube video?

Write a one-line brief, then six parts: a hook, a promise that matches your title, a short setup, three to five chapters that each open with a mini-hook, a payoff and one closing line. Script the hook, promise and close word for word. Read it aloud, cut lines you trip on and check the word count against your target length.

### How many words is a 10-minute YouTube script?

About 1,500 words at 150 words per minute, or 1,300 if your video has demos and pauses. A 5-minute script is 650 to 750 words and a 15-minute script is 1,950 to 2,250.

### What is the 30-second rule on YouTube?

It is creator shorthand for the idea that the first 30 seconds decide whether people stay. It lines up with YouTube Analytics, which reports an "Intro" figure for the share of viewers still watching after 30 seconds. YouTube's suggestion for improving it is to make the opening match the title and thumbnail.

### Should I script my YouTube videos word for word?

Script the hook, the promise and the closing line word for word. For the middle, write a full script for tutorials and video essays, where wording and order matter, and bullet points for opinion, commentary and stories, where you want to sound unrehearsed.

### What is the rule of three in script writing?

It is the habit of grouping points, examples or beats in threes, because three is easy to follow and remember. In a YouTube script it often shows up as three chapters or three fixes promised in the hook. Use it when you actually have three things; do not invent a third.

### How do you format a YouTube script?

Use two columns or two labels per section: what you say, and what the viewer sees (B-roll, screen recording, on-screen text). Add a chapter title above each section so you can copy them into the description as timestamps. The template above is a ready-made version.

### Can AI write a YouTube script?

It can draft one. You still need to check every fact, replace generic examples with your own, cut the stock openers and read it aloud for length and rhythm. The checklist in the AI section above covers the edit.

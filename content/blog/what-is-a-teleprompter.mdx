A teleprompter is a screen that shows a script to someone speaking on camera or on stage, placed so they can read it while looking at the lens or the audience. In the classic setup, a monitor lies flat under the camera and shows the text mirrored. A sheet of angled, partly reflective glass in front of the lens reflects the words up to the speaker's eyes, and the camera films straight through the glass without seeing the text.

That is the version used in TV studios. Most creators now use a simpler kind: an app that scrolls the script on a laptop, phone or second screen right next to the camera. It works on the same idea, keeping the words as close to the lens as possible, and it is what this guide spends most of its time on.

## Teleprompter meaning

The word comes from the TelePrompTer Corporation, the company that built the first commercial machines. Its prompter was first used live on December 4, 1950, on the CBS soap opera "The First Hundred Years" ([HISTORY](https://www.history.com/articles/teleprompter-invention-television-politics)). Today "teleprompter" is the general name for any device that scrolls a script for a speaker. In the UK you will also hear "autocue", the name of a British prompter brand.

You will also see it called a prompter, a cue screen or a script reader. They all mean the same thing.

## How does a teleprompter work?

A camera-mounted teleprompter has four parts:

| Part                 | What it does                                                                                                                              |
| -------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Monitor              | Lies face up under the lens and shows the script, flipped left to right                                                                   |
| Beam-splitter glass  | Sits at about 45° in front of the lens. It reflects the monitor up to you and lets most of the light from your face through to the camera |
| Hood                 | A fabric or plastic shroud that blocks light around the lens so the camera does not pick up reflections                                   |
| Software and control | Scrolls the text. The speed is set in advance, adjusted by a remote or operator, or follows your voice                                    |

The glass does the trick. It is coated so that part of the light passes through and part bounces off. Autocue's beam splitters, for example, let 70% of light through and reflect 30% ([Autocue](https://www.autocue.com/education/tutorials/how-a-prompter-works/)), and Elgato lists the same 70/30 split for its Prompter ([Elgato](https://www.elgato.com/us/en/p/prompter)). From your side you see bright text floating over the lens. From the camera's side the glass is just a slightly dark window, so the recording shows your face and none of the words.

The text is mirrored on the monitor because a reflection reverses it. Flip it once in software and it reads the right way round in the glass. Every teleprompter app has a mirror setting for this reason, and forgetting to switch it on (or off, when you use the app without glass) is the most common first-day mistake.

Because the words sit directly over the lens, your eyes point almost exactly at the camera while you read. That is the whole point: the viewer feels you are talking to them rather than glancing at notes.

## Types of teleprompter

| Type                          | How it looks                                                           | Who uses it                                    | Typical cost                                                                                         |
| ----------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Camera-mounted beam splitter  | A hooded box with angled glass in front of the lens                    | TV studios, YouTubers with a dedicated setup   | From a few hundred dollars. Elgato's 9-inch Prompter lists at $299.99; broadcast rigs cost much more |
| Conference ("presidential")   | Two clear glass panes on stands either side of a lectern               | Politicians, keynote speakers                  | Usually rented with an operator for the event                                                        |
| Phone or tablet beam splitter | A small glass frame that clips in front of a phone or camera           | Solo creators who film with a phone            | Often under $100, plus an app                                                                        |
| On-screen app (no glass)      | The script scrolls in a narrow window on your laptop, phone or monitor | Creators, course makers, people on video calls | Free to subscription; you only need the device you already have                                      |
| Confidence monitor            | A screen on the floor or behind the camera showing notes or slides     | Stage speakers, live events                    | Varies; often part of the venue's AV setup                                                           |

The conference prompters you see beside a president's podium work the same way as the studio kind, minus the camera. A monitor at the base of each stand points up, and the glass reflects the text toward the speaker. The audience sees two panes of tinted glass. Having one on each side lets the speaker turn left and right as if addressing different parts of the room ([Autocue](https://www.autocue.com/education/tutorials/how-a-prompter-works/)).

Prices change, so check current listings. The Elgato price was checked on October 26, 2026.

## Do you need the glass? How far your eyes really move

If you use an app on a laptop, there is no mirror. Your eyes look at the screen, not the lens, and the camera can see that. How much it shows depends on two distances: how far the text is from the lens, and how far you are from the screen.

Here is the angle your eyes turn away from the lens in a few common setups. It is simple geometry (the arctangent of the offset divided by your distance), worked out for this guide:

| Setup                                                                 | Text offset from lens | Your distance | Eye angle away from lens |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------- | ------------- | ------------------------ |
| Laptop, script in the middle of the screen                            | 10 cm                 | 60 cm         | about 9.5°               |
| Laptop, script in a narrow window right under the webcam              | 5 cm                  | 60 cm         | about 4.8°               |
| Phone on a tripod, script at the top of the screen next to the camera | 2 cm                  | 40 cm         | about 2.9°               |
| Monitor behind the camera, narrow column, you sit further back        | 4 cm                  | 150 cm        | about 1.5°               |
| Beam-splitter glass                                                   | about 0 cm            | any           | about 0°                 |

Two things follow. First, halving the distance between the text and the lens halves the angle, so a narrow window pushed up against the webcam does more than any other setting. Second, sitting further back helps a lot, which is why studio presenters stand a couple of metres from the camera.

Width matters as much as height. If each line of text is 15 cm wide and you sit 60 cm away, your eyes sweep about 14° from side to side on every line, and viewers notice that sooner than a slight downward look. Keep the window narrow, around four to six words per line, so your eyes mostly move down rather than across.

## How the script scrolls

Teleprompters move the text in one of three ways:

- **Fixed speed.** You set a pace and the text rolls at that rate. Simple, but it does not wait if you pause or stumble.
- **Manual control.** A person, a foot pedal, a remote or a keyboard moves the text as you go. On news and live events, this is often a dedicated prompter operator.
- **Voice-following.** The app listens and scrolls as you speak, slowing down and pausing when you do. Elgato's Voice Sync is one example ([Elgato](https://www.elgato.com/us/en/p/prompter)). It is useful for long scripts, but it can lose its place if you ad-lib.

For a fixed speed, start near your natural speaking rate. The National Center for Voice and Speech puts conversational English at about 150 words per minute ([NCVS](https://ncvs.org/tutorials/voice-qualities/)). Many people read a script more slowly than that with pauses. Do one test take, and if you find yourself chasing the text, turn the speed down. If you are waiting for it, turn it up.

## How to read a teleprompter without looking like you are reading

Most of the advice creators give each other comes down to a few habits:

1. **Write for the prompter.** One idea per line, short sentences, and a blank line where you want a pause. A paragraph that reads well on paper is hard to say on camera. [How to write a video script](/blog/how-to-write-a-video-script) shows the same opening written both ways.
2. **Make the text big and the window small.** Three or four lines on screen at once is enough. More lines mean more eye travel.
3. **Keep your reading line near the top.** Read from the line closest to the lens, not from the middle of the screen.
4. **Read a little ahead.** Glance at the next line while you finish the current one, the way you would read a story to someone. It keeps your voice from going flat at the end of each line.
5. **Pause on purpose.** Stop at full stops and between sections. A pause also gives you a clean place to cut if you redo a line.
6. **Record in chunks.** Film one section at a time instead of the whole script in one take. If you stumble, pause and say the full line again rather than restarting from the top.
7. **Do a 15-second test.** Record a few lines, play them back and watch only your eyes. Then adjust the window position, text size or speed.

Here is a short script formatted for a prompter. Each line is one breath, and the bracketed notes are for you, not for reading aloud:

```text
Your eyes are giving away that you're reading.

[pause]

Here's the fix.

Shrink the script window.
Push it right up under the camera.

[point at the webcam]

Now your eyes barely move,
and nobody can tell.
```

## A short history of the teleprompter

Broadway actor Fred Barton Jr. wanted a way to stop worrying about lines as live television took off. With Irving Kahn, then at 20th Century Fox, and engineer Hubert Schlafly, he built the first teleprompter: a roll of paper typed in large letters, housed in a suitcase and unrolled near the camera. The patent was filed in 1949 and granted in 1953. In 1955 inventor Luther George Simjian patented an in-camera version, which let speakers read while looking down the lens. Harry Truman was among the first politicians to use one, for his 1951 State of the Union address, and by the 1970s and 1980s teleprompters were everywhere in politics ([HISTORY](https://www.history.com/articles/teleprompter-invention-television-politics)).

The basic idea has not changed since: put the words where the speaker's eyes need to be.

## Common questions

### What is a teleprompter used for?

To let someone deliver a script word for word while looking at the camera or audience. News anchors, politicians, keynote speakers, corporate presenters and creators use them for anything where the wording matters or the script is too long to memorize.

### What does a teleprompter look like?

A camera teleprompter looks like a hooded box in front of the lens with a sheet of tinted glass inside, angled at about 45°. A conference teleprompter looks like two clear glass panes on thin stands either side of a podium. An app teleprompter is just a scrolling text window on a laptop, phone or tablet.

### What does a teleprompter look like to the speaker?

You see bright lines of text that seem to float on the glass, with the dark camera lens faintly visible behind them. With an app on a laptop or phone, you see a scrolling text window, ideally placed right next to the camera.

### How does a presidential teleprompter work?

It is a conference prompter. A monitor at the base of each stand faces up and shows the speech, and an angled pane of glass on top reflects the text toward the speaker. The audience sees two pieces of tinted glass. An operator scrolls the text to match the speaker, and the two panes let the speaker turn left and right while still reading.

### Can the audience or camera see the teleprompter text?

No. The text is reflected toward the speaker, and the camera looks through the glass from behind, so it records the speaker without the words. At a live event, the audience sees the glass panes but not the script on them.

### How much does a teleprompter cost?

A teleprompter app can be free or part of a subscription. Clip-on glass for phones is often under $100, compact camera units like Elgato's Prompter list at around $300, and broadcast or conference systems cost far more and are usually rented with an operator.

### What do news anchors read from?

Usually a camera-mounted teleprompter on each studio camera, showing the script from the newsroom system. In many studios a prompter operator scrolls the text at the anchor's pace, and printed scripts on the desk serve as a backup.

### Who controls the teleprompter speed?

It depends on the setup. In a studio or at a live event, a prompter operator often scrolls the text to match the speaker. Solo creators set a fixed speed, use a remote or foot pedal, or use voice-following that scrolls as they talk.

### Is it hard to read from a teleprompter?

It takes a few sessions to sound natural. The common problems are a flat voice, eyes visibly moving across long lines, and chasing text that scrolls too fast. Short lines, a narrow window, a slower speed and a quick test take solve most of them.

### What is the difference between a teleprompter and an autocue?

There is none in practice. Autocue is a brand of prompter, and in British English the brand name became the everyday word for the device, the same way "TelePrompTer" did in the US.

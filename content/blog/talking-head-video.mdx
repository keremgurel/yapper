A talking head video shows a real person speaking directly to the camera, usually framed from the head and shoulders or chest up. It is used for tutorials, course lessons, product updates, reviews and YouTube explainers. Unlike screen recordings that show a desktop, interviews with multiple people, or AI-generated avatars, a talking head puts one person in front of the lens to talk through their message.

This guide covers everything from planning the shot to adding captions, with an original 60-second example script and a copyable shot sheet.

## How talking heads differ from other video formats

Here is how talking-head videos compare to a few formats that are sometimes confused with them:

| Format                | What it shows                                             | Who speaks                       | Common use                          |
| --------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| Talking head          | One person facing the camera                              | That person                      | Tutorials, updates, reviews         |
| Screen recording      | Desktop, app or browser with optional picture-in-picture  | One person (voice or on-screen)  | Software walkthroughs, demos        |
| Interview             | Two or more people                                        | Both                             | Podcasts, documentaries, Q&A        |
| AI avatar             | Computer-generated character or synthetic face            | Synthetic voice or voice clone   | Automated content, language dubbing |

Talking-head videos can include B-roll (cutaways that show what you are describing) and screen inserts, but the person addressing the viewer stays in the frame and drives the message.

## Minimum setup: what you already have

You do not need to buy gear before your first take. Start with what you have and test it:

- **Camera.** A phone or the webcam in your Mac. Recent devices typically record at 1080p or higher, which is sufficient for most platforms.
- **Tripod or support.** Anything stable that places the lens at your eye level. A stack of books under a laptop or a phone holder works.
- **Room.** A quiet space with soft furniture. Hard walls and floors reflect sound, creating echo.
- **Light.** A window or a ceiling light. Place the main light in front of you and slightly to one side, not behind you.
- **Microphone.** Start with the built-in mic. Move it closer to your mouth if you can, and listen through headphones to check for echo and background noise before you record the full take.

Record a 20-second test, watch it back with the sound on, and note what you want to fix. Buying something makes sense only after you know what the test revealed.

## Recording checklist: seven things to check before you hit record

Work through this list once before each take. It catches the most common avoidable mistakes:

1. **Frame for the format.** Vertical (9:16) for Reels, Shorts and TikTok; horizontal (16:9) for YouTube and courses. Leave space above your head (about a hand's width), and frame low enough to show your hands if you gesture.
2. **Check the background.** A plain wall or a tidy shelf in soft focus is better than a cluttered desk or an unmade bed in sharp detail. Increase the distance between you and the background, or use a wider aperture (lower f-number) if your camera allows manual control to blur it.
3. **Test the light.** Sit in position and check your face on the screen. If one side is much darker than the other, move closer to the window or add a light on the dark side. If the picture is dim overall, face the main light source.
4. **Confirm the mic is live and close.** Speak at normal volume and watch the meter or listen through headphones. If the levels barely move, check that you selected the right input or move the mic closer.
5. **Put the script near the lens.** If you are reading from a [teleprompter](/blog/what-is-a-teleprompter), place the text window as close to the webcam as you can and make the column narrow, about four to six words per line. Wide text makes your eyes sweep left and right on each line, and viewers notice that.
6. **Set your eye line.** Look just above or just below the lens if the camera is at eye level, or directly into it if you are using a beam-splitter teleprompter. A downward glance to notes is natural; a sideways look to a second screen is not.
7. **Do a 10-second test take.** Record, play it back, and listen for echo, check the framing, and confirm the light looks even. Fix what you find, then record the real take.

This checklist assumes you are recording yourself. If someone else is operating the camera, add focus and exposure checks.

## Example: an original 60-second script

This example shows a creator explaining a simple two-step desk reset. It is a clearly fictional demonstration, not a real customer story or a testimonial.

The script follows the hook → problem → solution → close structure covered in [how to write a video script](/blog/how-to-write-a-video-script):

```text
If your desk is tidy but you still lose your notes,
try a two-minute reset before you close your laptop.

[pause]

Here is the problem.
Ideas show up in messages, notebooks, sticky notes and voice memos.
When they live in five places, none of them gets checked.

[pause]

Step one: choose one capture place.
I use a single notebook.
Everything that comes in during the day goes on one page,
and I review it at the end.

Step two: write the next action before you close the computer.
Not the whole project.
Just the one thing you will do when you open it again tomorrow.

[pause]

Two minutes, two steps.
Your desk stays clear and your notes stay in one place.
Try it tonight and let me know how it works.
```

That is 128 spoken words, excluding `[pause]` markers. At 150 words per minute, it runs about 51 seconds before pauses. At a slower pace of 120 words per minute, it runs about 64 seconds before pauses.

No productivity statistic or customer performance claim is included, because those numbers require measurement and verification.

## Shot sheet: visuals to match the script

A shot sheet lists what the camera shows during each part of the script. It separates the stage directions from the words you read on the teleprompter, so you know when to pick something up or turn to the screen.

Here is the shot sheet for the script above:

| Shot | Spoken line / description                                                                      | Estimated duration | Visual action                                                |
| ---- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| 1    | Hook: "If your desk is tidy but you still lose your notes, try a two-minute reset…"           | 7 seconds          | A-roll: speaker faces camera, gestures to tidy desk          |
| 2    | Problem: "Ideas show up in messages, notebooks, sticky notes and voice memos…"                 | 12 seconds         | Close-up: hand placing sticky notes on desk and phone screen |
| 3    | Step one: "Choose one capture place. I use a single notebook…"                                 | 11 seconds         | Close-up: open notebook, pen writing a short note            |
| 4    | Step two: "Write the next action before you close the computer. Just the one thing…"           | 14 seconds         | Screen insert: text cursor typing one action in a task list  |
| 5    | Close: "Two minutes, two steps. Your desk stays clear and your notes stay in one place…"       | 9 seconds          | A-roll: return to speaker, satisfied expression              |

The durations are estimates based on a reading pace of about 150 words per minute and the length of each section. The actual timing will vary depending on your pace, pauses and how long each visual action takes to show on screen.

A-roll means the main footage of the person speaking. B-roll or inserts are the supporting shots.

## Full script, bullets or memorization?

The method you use to keep your place depends on the video's length, how well you know the material, and whether you will edit the take afterward:

- **Full script on a teleprompter.** Best for videos where the exact wording matters, such as product claims, sponsor reads, or any script that has been approved by someone else. Also useful when the video is longer than about three minutes and you have not recorded it before.
- **Talking points or bullet lists.** Best for videos on topics you know well and explain often. A short list of three to five points keeps you on track without making you sound like you are reading.
- **Memorized sections.** Best for short videos under about a minute, where you want to move freely or use your hands. [How to memorize a script](/blog/how-to-memorize-a-script) covers learning it quickly.

You can combine these. Write the hook and the close word for word, because those are the parts where exact wording matters most, and use bullets for the middle.

## Recording in one take or in segments?

Both approaches work. Choose based on the video's structure and how you prefer to work:

**One continuous take** gives you natural pacing and continuity. Your tone and energy stay consistent, and you do not have to match lighting or performance across multiple clips. Use this when the video is short, you know the material well, or the script flows as one thought.

**Recording in segments** lets you get each section exactly right and makes the edit easier. You can redo one paragraph without repeating the whole video, and you can shoot B-roll or demonstrations between sections. Use this when the video includes multiple topics, precise explanations, or sections where you show something on screen.

If you stumble during a continuous take, pause for two seconds, then restart the complete sentence and keep going. That pause gives you a clear marker in the waveform, and restarting the sentence gives you a clean point to cut. Save the best complete take rather than trying to assemble the perfect line from ten attempts; it is faster to edit, and the pacing sounds more natural.

## Recording with a teleprompter: practical habits

A teleprompter shows your script so you can read while looking near the camera. It does not eliminate pauses, fix awkward phrasing, or solve anxiety automatically. Those are habits you build by recording and reviewing takes. Here is what does help:

- **Break the script into thought units.** Write one idea per line, with a blank line or `[pause]` between beats. Reading "Here is the problem" as its own line, then pausing, then starting the next thought is easier than reading three sentences in one visual block.
- **Set the text size so you can read it instantly.** If you have to lean forward or squint, make it bigger.
- **Keep the column narrow.** A wide block of text makes your eyes sweep side to side. Four to six words per line keeps the movement mostly vertical, which is less visible to viewers.
- **Place the text as close to the lens as you can.** On a Mac, position the teleprompter window just below the webcam. The closer the text is to the lens, the smaller the angle your eyes turn away from the camera.
- **Test the scroll speed with a 20-second read.** Many people set the speed too fast at first. If you are chasing the text, slow it down. Conversational English averages about 150 words per minute ([NCVS](https://ncvs.org/tutorials/voice-qualities/)), and many people read scripts more slowly than that with pauses.
- **Rehearse aloud before you record.** Reading silently does not show you where the script trips your tongue or where a sentence is too long to say in one breath.

The teleprompter keeps the words in front of you, but your delivery still depends on a script written to be spoken, adequate rehearsal, and a pace that lets you think between lines. ## Editing and captions: finishing the video

After recording, the editing workflow for a talking-head video usually follows this order:

1. **Choose the best take.** If you recorded more than one version of the same section, pick the one with the clearest delivery and the fewest mistakes.
2. **Cut out pauses, stumbles and repeated attempts.** Remove the false starts and the long silences, but keep the natural pauses that give viewers time to absorb a point. A text-based editor makes this faster: you select and delete the words you do not want, and the video cuts match the transcript.
3. **Insert B-roll or screen recordings where they support the message.** A close-up of a notebook or a screen showing a task list gives viewers something to look at while you describe the action. Keep these inserts short—three to five seconds—and cut back to your face to maintain connection.
4. **Generate and review the captions.** Automatic captions are a starting point, not a finished product. Check that names, numbers, and negations ("can" versus "can't") are correct, and ensure that line breaks land at natural pauses rather than mid-phrase. The W3C's guidance is clear: captions must accurately represent the spoken content and meaningful non-speech sounds, and automatically generated captions require review to be accessible ([W3C Media Accessibility](https://www.w3.org/WAI/media/av/captions/)).
5. **Check that overlays and text do not hide the captions.** Lower-third titles, logos and outro cards should sit above or beside the caption area, not on top of it.

Do not cut every breath or smooth out every pause. Talking-head videos sound natural when they sound like a real person speaking, and that includes the small hesitations that show thinking.

## Troubleshooting common issues

Here are the problems that come up most often when recording talking-head videos, with the fix for each:

| Problem                   | Likely cause                                          | Fix                                                                                              |
| ------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Face is too dark          | Light source is behind you or to the side             | Turn your desk toward the window, or place a lamp in front of you and slightly to one side       |
| Echo in the audio         | Room has hard walls, floors and no soft furniture     | Record in a smaller room with carpet, curtains or a couch, or move the mic closer to your mouth  |
| Eyes look down or sideways| Script is too far from the lens, or screen is too wide| Move the teleprompter window closer to the webcam and make the text column narrower              |
| Delivery sounds robotic   | Script is written for reading, not speaking           | Rewrite the lines the way you would say them in conversation, add pauses, and rehearse aloud     |
| Editing feels tedious     | Long continuous take with many mistakes               | Record in shorter sections, restart the sentence when you stumble, and leave handles around cuts |
| Captions are inaccurate   | Automatic transcription missed specialized terms      | Manually correct names, brand terms and numbers; save corrected spellings for future videos      |

Use this as a final checklist before you export:

- [ ] The message is clear and matches the brief
- [ ] The audio is clean and free from echo or background noise
- [ ] Your face is lit evenly and in focus
- [ ] B-roll and screen inserts support the message and do not distract
- [ ] Captions are accurate, with correct spelling and line breaks at natural pauses
- [ ] The video has a clear ending: a next step, a question or a call to action

That checklist catches the issues that affect whether someone watches to the end and whether they understand the message.

## Common questions about talking-head videos

### Can I record a talking-head video on my phone?

Yes. Recent phone cameras typically record at 1080p or higher, which is sufficient quality for Reels, Shorts, TikTok and YouTube. Use a tripod or stable surface to hold it at eye level, test the sound in the room you will use, and place a light source in front of you. The main limitation is screen size: a phone's smaller display makes it harder to read a teleprompter script from a distance, so you may need to use talking points or memorize short sections instead.

### Do I need a teleprompter for a talking-head video?

No. A teleprompter is useful when you need to deliver exact wording, when the script is long, or when you are recording something unfamiliar. Many creators use bullet points or memorize short videos instead. For a 30- to 60-second video on a topic you know well, talking points often work better than reading, because they let you sound more natural.

### How long should a talking-head video be?

Choose the length that fits your message. The right length depends on what you need to say and where you will post it. A three-minute video that delivers value holds attention better than a ten-minute video with filler.

### Should I record talking-head videos in portrait or landscape?

Choose the aspect ratio based on where you will post the video. Portrait (9:16) is the default for Reels, Shorts and TikTok. Landscape (16:9) is standard for longer YouTube videos, courses and presentations. If you plan to post to multiple platforms, record in the format that fits your primary destination. Cropping landscape footage into portrait works when you framed for it, but you lose the sides of the image. Cropping portrait into landscape adds black bars or forces a tight crop that can cut off your head or gestures.

### Is it better to record one long take or short clips?

One continuous take works well when the video is short, the message flows as one thought, and you want natural pacing and energy. Recording in segments works better when the video has distinct sections, includes B-roll or demonstrations, or when you want to get each part exactly right. You can also combine the two: record the main explanation in one take and capture close-ups or screen inserts separately. If you stumble during a long take, pause and restart the sentence rather than stopping the recording; it is faster to cut the mistake in the edit than to match multiple full takes.

### What is the difference between a talking-head video and an AI avatar video?

A talking-head video shows a real person speaking to the camera. An AI avatar video shows a computer-generated character or a synthetic version of a person's likeness, often driven by text-to-speech or voice cloning. Talking-head videos build trust through direct, authentic communication. AI avatars are used for automated content, language dubbing and situations where showing a real person is impractical.
